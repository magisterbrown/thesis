\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

This chapter draws the results of the thesis together. It summarises the main
findings, answers the research questions posed in
Section~\ref{sec:research_questions}, states the limitations of the study, and
outlines directions for future work.

\section{Main Findings}
\label{sec:main_findings}

For type-1 transforms the time was concentrated in one place. The
\texttt{execute} stage, which spreads the points, transforms the fine grid and
corrects for the kernel, took the large majority of it; \texttt{setpts}, which
sorts the points, took a smaller but non-negligible share; and \texttt{makeplan}
took almost none. A type-3 transform divided its time differently, because the
inner plan it needs and its per-target correction factors are both built in
\texttt{setpts}. Every parameter studied here acted inside the first two of those
stages.


Each parameter moved the total, and the parameters were found to be of two kinds. 
Four of them -- the upsampling factor, the maximum subproblem size and the two bin dimensions -- reduced the cost of one stage only by raising the cost of another. The cost as a function of such a parameter is a sum of two opposing terms, so the best value lay where they balanced rather than at either extreme. The remaining two did not balance two
stages against each other. Sorting is a binary choice, and it should be enabled
for any input of a realistic size. The atomic-update threshold is an integer that may take any value, but the library compares it with the thread count and nothing else. All it selects is which of two implementations of the adding stage runs: a critical section when the thread count does not exceed it, atomic updates when it does. Its effect is therefore a step rather than a
balance, and only its position relative to the thread count matters.

Where those balance points lie is a property of the problem and of the machine,
not of the library, and the quantities that fix them are few. The point density
decides how large a region a subproblem's points cover, and so whether writing a
subgrid or filling the subproblem buffer with points dominates. The requested tolerance affects the kernel width,
and with it both the work per point and the padding around every subgrid. The
dimension multiplies that padding along every axis. The thread count changes the
fraction of the L3 cache each thread has and how many threads contend for memory at once. The thresholds these produced were sharp, and each could be located in advance. Gathering cost more once the staging buffer outgrows a thread's share of the last-level cache. The sort moved little beyond its compulsory traffic while its per-thread counter array fitted in that same share, and spreading left its flat regime once the region a bin covers no longer fitted in the first-level cache. The distances involved were not small. For the
reference transform the subproblem size the library selects moved $8.2$ lines
per point between the processor and memory in the spreading stage, against
$6.4$ at a value twelve times smaller, and over the $54$ test transforms the
sort took $2.2$ times as long with the bins it fixes as with the selected ones.

The behaviour therefore separated into a part that could be predicted and a part
that had to be measured. What follows from the geometry of the grids was predictable. The average size of the subgrid a given subproblem size and bin shape produce was derived in closed form and matched measurement to within one percent. The adding stage cost a fixed number of cycles per cell written, so that model also predicted the cost of the stage for configurations never run.
The cost of spreading as a function of the upsampling factor was reproduced in
all three dimensions. What follows from the memory hierarchy was predictable only in part. The position of each threshold followed from a cache size, but the cost of crossing it did not. The model of the FFT was narrower still, holding only for transforms whose fine grid exceeds the last-level cache; below that a fixed per-call overhead dominated.

For the spreader parameters the predictable part was enough to choose a configuration. A model of the memory traffic of the bin sort and the spreading stage, evaluated over the candidate bins and subproblem sizes that the cache limits allow, selected both parameters before the transform ran. On $20$ threads the selection was $1.50$ times faster than the library defaults on geometric mean over $54$ transforms and slower on only three. The larger bins made the sort $2.2$ times faster, and the selected subproblem size reduced the CPU time of the spreading stage by about $7\%$. For the upsampling factor no such procedure was proposed, because the FFT cost it would have to weigh could be ranked but not predicted.

These findings are consistent with, and extend, the prior work reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:related_work}. The trade-off behind the upsampling factor agrees with what gNUFFTW~\cite{ou2017} found on a GPU, that the best oversampling ratio falls as the point density falls; where gNUFFTW located that optimum by search, this thesis modelled the spreading side of it and explained why it moves. The analysis of Beatty et al.~\cite{beatty2005} and Barnett~\cite{barnett2021aliasing} is what makes this comparison possible: because every upsampling factor in the interval studied reaches the requested accuracy, the factors can be compared on cost alone. The structural parameters show the largest contrast with earlier work. gNUFFTW left the sector width of gpuNUFFT out of its search, yet the corresponding parameters in FINUFFT, the bin dimensions, accounted for most of the gain of the selection procedure. The cache limit on the padded bin found here appears to be the CPU counterpart of the shared-memory limit that bounds the bins of cuFINUFFT~\cite{shih2021}, with the difference that exceeding it makes spreading slower rather than impossible.

The results have practical implications for FINUFFT and libraries like it. The bin dimensions, which carry most of the gain, are compile-time constants in the released library; exposing them as options, or choosing them per transform, would be a small change to the code. Because the selection procedure evaluates only closed-form expressions and takes well under a millisecond, it could run during planning without the cost of a search, which makes it suitable even for transforms that are executed only once. The benefit is largest for multi-threaded transforms, where spreading is limited by memory bandwidth, and smallest on few threads and in three dimensions. The same reasoning may apply to cuFINUFFT and other GPU implementations, with shared memory taking the place of the first-level cache, although this was not tested.

\section{Answers to the Research Questions}
\label{sec:rq_answers}

\paragraph{RQ1: Which of FINUFFT's configuration parameters affect performance
significantly on the machine under test?}
Six of the options the library carries reach the stages in which the time is spent, and all six changed the execution time of a transform: whether the nonuniform points are sorted (\texttt{spread\_sort}), the upsampling factor (\texttt{upsampfac}), the maximum number of points in a spreading subproblem (\texttt{spread\_max\_sp\_size}), the thread count above which the adding stage switches from a critical section to atomic updates (\texttt{spread\_nthr\_atomic}), and the dimensions of the boxes into which the points are sorted ($b_x$, $b_y$, $b_z$), which the released library fixes at compile time. On the reference transform,
sorting accounted for $99.5\%$ of the variance in execution time across the
search. Among configurations that sort, the upsampling factor accounted for
$53.8\%$, the two bin dimensions for $32.2\%$ and $9.5\%$, the maximum
subproblem size for $3.2\%$ and the atomic-update threshold for $1.3\%$
(Chapter~\ref{ch:parameter_importance}). A low share does not mean a small
effect: the best configuration found kept $\sigma$ and sorting at their
defaults, yet was $23.3\%$ faster than the defaults, entirely through the
maximum subproblem size, the bin dimensions and the atomic-update threshold.

Four of the six sped up one stage and slowed another, so each had an interior optimum; sorting and the atomic-update threshold selected between alternatives instead. The upsampling factor traded spreading against the FFT: raising $\sigma$ narrowed the kernel, making spreading cheaper, and enlarged the fine grid the FFT must transform (Chapter~\ref{ch:sigma_selection}). The maximum subproblem size traded gathering against adding: adding fell as subproblems grew, because the padding of every subgrid is paid once per subproblem, while gathering rose once the per-thread staging buffer no longer fits in that thread's share of L3. The bin dimensions acted on three stages: the sort through the size of its per-thread counter array against that thread's share of L3, spreading through the padded region a bin covers against L1 and L2, and adding through the size of the subgrids the resulting point order produces (Chapter~\ref{ch:parameter_tuning}).

\paragraph{RQ2: Can models describe how changing each parameter affects the
cost of the individual stages of the transform?} For the upsampling factor, the
maximum subproblem size and the bin dimensions, yes, though not all to the same
degree. The subgrid a configuration produces was predicted to
within a percent, and with it the cost of the adding stage, which was
proportional to the cells written; the cost of spreading as a function of the
upsampling factor was reproduced in all three dimensions. The costs that follow from the memory hierarchy were located by thresholds, each given by the capacity of a cache level divided by the bytes a stage holds per unit of the parameter: the maximum subproblem size at which the staging buffer outgrows a thread's share of the last-level cache; the bin dimensions at which the counter array the sort keeps per thread outgrows that same share; and the bin dimensions at which the region a bin covers, padded by the kernel width, leaves the first-level cache. The first two entered a closed-form estimate of the DRAM lines the bin sort and the spreading stage move per point, through ramps whose ends are fitted once on the machine; on the $54$ test transforms its median error was $7\%$, and it ranked configurations better than it predicted their absolute traffic. The third was used as a hard limit on the bins rather than costed. Ranking is what choosing requires, and it held wherever spreading was bound by bandwidth, which on this machine means from about ten threads upwards. The model of the FFT cost was the weakest, holding only
where the fine grid exceeds the last-level cache, so for the upsampling factor it ranked candidates but did not predict their cost.

Because the models are written in terms of the point density, tolerance, dimension, thread count and cache sizes rather than measured times, they are expected to carry over to other machines. This was not tested: all measurements were taken on one machine, and the fitted ramp ends would need to be measured again on another (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{RQ3: Can such models choose a better configuration before a
transform runs?} The models first showed why there was room to improve on the
defaults. The default bins of
$16 \times 4$ cells left a per-thread histogram of $2.85$\,MB on the reference
transform, against a $2.5$\,MB share of L3 on $20$ threads, so the sort was
already past its cache threshold; in one dimension the histogram ran to one or
two million counters and overflowed every level of the cache. The default
$S_{\max} = 10^5$ moved $8.2$ lines per point between the processor and memory,
against $6.4$ at $S_{\max} = 8000$, where the stage was fastest. In three
dimensions, by contrast, even the default bin exceeded the first-level cache once
padded, so no larger bin was admissible and the procedure kept it. The upsampling factor
heuristic considers only $1.25$ and $2$, although the balance between spreading
and the FFT was often found to lie between them.

For the bin dimensions and the maximum subproblem size, the models also chose a
better configuration. The selection procedure of Chapter~\ref{ch:parameter_tuning}
used only the dimension, precision, grid size, number of points, kernel width,
thread count and cache sizes, and took well under a millisecond. On $20$ threads
it was faster than the defaults on $51$ of $54$ transforms and $1.50$ times faster
on geometric mean, with the largest gains in one dimension ($2.05$ to $2.15$
times) and the smallest in three ($1.08$ to $1.16$ times), where no bin larger
than the default fitted in the first-level cache and only $S_{\max}$ changed. The
gains shrank with the thread count, to $1.23$ times on ten threads and $1.07$ on
one, because spreading was then limited by latency and computation rather than by
the traffic the model counts. With exponentially distributed points the procedure
was still $1.40$ times faster overall, but three-dimensional transforms became
slower in $15$ of $18$ cases. For the upsampling factor the question was left
open: its spreading cost was modelled accurately, but its FFT cost was not
predicted well enough to weigh against it.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The measurements throughout were taken on one machine, the Intel Xeon E5-2698 v4 of Section~\ref{sec:platform}, and the thresholds that turn out to matter most are expressed in terms of its cache sizes. The models are written in terms of those sizes rather than in absolute numbers, so they transfer in principle, but this was not verified on hardware with a different cache hierarchy, a different core count, or a memory system of a different bandwidth. Two of the mechanisms identified here depend on more than the cache sizes alone. The point at which the staging buffer leaves a thread's share of the last-level cache depends on how many threads share that cache, and the cost of memory contention at large subproblem sizes depends on the bandwidth available to each thread.

The parameter search of Chapter~\ref{ch:parameter_importance} likewise covers a single workload; its ranking of the parameters is specific to that workload, and the analysis chapters show directly that a parameter which is insensitive at one density and thread count is decisive at another. The analyses concentrate on type-1 transforms, mostly in double precision, since that is the path the spreader takes; type-2 interpolation reuses the same subproblem structure but was not measured separately, and type~3 was not examined beyond the stage breakdown.

Two of the results also rest on a modified library rather than on FINUFFT as distributed. The dimensions of the sort bins are compile-time constants, so they were exposed as run-time options in order to be swept at all. A user of the released library cannot set them, and the gains attributed to them in Chapter~\ref{ch:parameter_importance} are therefore available only through a patch, or through a future release that exposes them. The same holds for the selection procedure of Chapter~\ref{ch:parameter_tuning}, which chooses bin dimensions as well as $S_{\max}$.

The selection procedure has three limitations of its own. Its traffic estimate assumes uniformly distributed points, and with clustered points it fails in three dimensions, where it selects subproblems so large that each thread receives only a few of very different cost. It has no term for load imbalance at all, which is also the cause of its three slowdowns with uniform points. And the ramps that describe how traffic rises once a working set leaves its cache were fitted on this machine, so on another the procedure keeps its form but its constants would need to be measured again.
\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future_work}

The first continuation is to repeat the study on another machine. The models
are written in terms of cache sizes and thread counts rather than absolute
times, but they were validated, and the ends of the ramps in the traffic
estimate were fitted, on a single processor. Repeating the measurements on one
with a different cache hierarchy, core count and memory bandwidth would test
whether the thresholds move where the models say they should, and whether the
selection procedure keeps its gains once its constants are refitted there.

The second is a model for interpolation, the counterpart of spreading used by
type-2 transforms and, through the inner transform it plans, by type-3
transforms. Interpolation shares the bin sort, the bin dimensions and the
staging buffer with spreading, but each point reads its neighbourhood directly
from the shared fine grid, so there are no private subgrids and no adding stage.
Its traffic estimate would therefore keep the terms of the sort and of the
staging buffer and replace the subgrid terms with the reads of the fine grid,
whose reuse is set by the region a bin covers. Whether the same thresholds
govern it, and whether a selection procedure built on such an estimate gains
as much as it does for spreading, remains to be measured.

\section{Closing Remarks}

Understanding why a configuration is fast has proven more useful than finding
it by search: it explains the defaults, shows where they fall short, and allows
better values to be chosen before a transform runs.
