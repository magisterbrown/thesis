\chapter{Selecting the Best Sigma Parameter}
\label{ch:sigma_selection}

\section{Overview}

The upsampling factor $\sigma$ is among the most impactful parameters in FINUFFT. It determines the size of the internal FFT grid relative to the output grid, and thus directly controls the memory footprint, cache behaviour, and compute cost of the transform. This chapter investigates how to select $\sigma$ optimally for a given input configuration and accuracy requirement.

\section{The Role of $\sigma$ in FINUFFT}

The upsampling factor $\sigma$ controls the size of the internal uniform FFT grid, which has $\sigma N$ points per dimension relative to the $N$ requested output modes. Increasing $\sigma$ enlarges the FFT grid, raising FFT cost, but simultaneously relaxes the accuracy requirement on the spreading kernel, permitting a smaller kernel half-width $w$. Because $w$ decreases and the grid size increases as $\sigma$ grows, there exists a value of $\sigma$ that minimises total transform cost for a given problem size, dimension, and tolerance.

\section{Current Heuristic for $\sigma$ Selection}
The current FINUFFT heuristic for $\sigma$ selection is a simple lookup: it returns one of only two possible values, $\sigma \in \{1.25, 2.00\}$. By default it returns $1.25$, but switches to $2.00$ when the point density $\rho = M/N$ exceeds a fixed threshold, or when the requested tolerance $\varepsilon$ is too tight for $\sigma = 1.25$ to reach. All upsampling factors in between are never considered, even though the true optimal $\sigma$ for a given problem often lies in the interior of $[1.25, 2.00]$.

\section{Cost Model}

\subsection{Estimating Spreading Cost}

The spreading step accumulates contributions from each of the $M$ non-uniform source points onto a $w^d$ neighbourhood of grid cells. The minimum kernel half-width $w$ required to achieve tolerance $\varepsilon$ at upsampling $\sigma$ in $d$ dimensions is
\begin{equation}
    w = \left\lceil \frac{\log\!\left(\dfrac{0.18 \cdot 1.4^{d-1}}{\varepsilon}\right)}{\pi \sqrt{1 - 1/\sigma}} + 1 \right\rceil.
    \label{eq:kernel_width}
\end{equation}
The theoretical spreading cost is therefore proportional to $M w^d$. In practice the inner loop is vectorised, so the per-point work scales as $\lceil w / W_{\mathrm{simd}} \rceil \cdot w^{d-1}$, where $W_{\mathrm{simd}}$ is the SIMD vector width in grid points.

\subsection{Estimating FFT Cost}

The FFT operates on the upsampled grid of total size $\tilde{N} = \mathrm{next235}(\sigma N)^d$, where $\mathrm{next235}(x)$ denotes the smallest integer $\geq x$ whose only prime factors are $2$, $3$, and $5$. Rounding up to such a highly composite number ensures the FFT can be decomposed into small radix-2, -3, and -5 butterflies, which most FFT libraries handle efficiently. The grid contributes $\mathcal{O}(\tilde{N} \log \tilde{N})$ operations, though the precise constant depends on the chosen FFT backend and cache effects, so this estimate is best treated as an asymptotic guide.

\section{Optimal $\sigma$ as a Function of Input}
\label{sec:cost_model_intro}

The total transform cost is the sum of both contributions,
\begin{equation}
    T(\sigma) \;\approx\; M\, w(\sigma,\varepsilon)^d \;+\; \gamma\, \tilde{N}(\sigma)\log \tilde{N}(\sigma),
    \label{eq:cost_model}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma = C_{\mathrm{FFT}} / C_{\mathrm{spread}}$ is a single hardware-dependent constant expressing the relative cost of one FFT butterfly versus one spreading operation. Since only the ratio matters for finding the optimal $\sigma$, the overall scale cancels.

\section{Fitting $\gamma$ from Measurements}
All timing measurements in this chapter were collected using Google Benchmark~\cite{googlebenchmark}. Google Benchmark provides statistical rigor by running each fixture repeatedly and reporting mean and standard deviation, automatically discarding warm-up iterations and compensating for clock resolution. It also integrates with hardware performance counters, enabling cycle-accurate comparisons across configurations. These properties make it well-suited for the fine-grained measurements of spreading and FFT cost required here.

To validate the $w^d$ scaling predicted by the model, spreading time was measured across one and two dimensions for a range of $\sigma$ values at tolerance $\varepsilon = 10^{-6}$. \subsection{Fitting the Balancing Constant $\gamma$}
\label{sec:gamma_fit}

The balancing constant $\gamma$ is the only free parameter in the cost model~\eqref{eq:cost_model}; it encodes the relative throughput of the FFT and spreading kernels on a given machine. Because this ratio is hardware-dependent and not analytically available, $\gamma$ must be calibrated empirically. Rather than a single machine-wide constant, $\gamma$ is fit separately for each of the six combinations of transform type (1 or 2)  and problem dimension (1, 2, or 3). This fit uses an independently gathered timing sweep spanning nine densities $\rho \in \{0.125, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32\}$  crossed with every tolerance decade the precision supports, $\varepsilon \in \{10^{-3}, \ldots, 10^{-9}\}$ for double and $\varepsilon \in \{10^{-3}, \ldots, 10^{-6}\}$ for float. For each $(\rho, \varepsilon)$ combination, the measurement itself is a throughput-versus-upsampling-factor sweep, stepping $\sigma$ across $[1.25, 2.00]$ in increments of $0.05$.


The raw timing curves susceptible to transient outliers caused by OS scheduling, cache interference, and other system noise. To reduce this effect, a 3-point median filter was applied to each timing curve along the $\sigma$ axis before any further analysis. Median smoothing was preferred over averaging because it suppresses isolated spikes without distorting the location of the minimum, which is the quantity of interest. Figure~\ref{fig:smoothing} illustrates the effect: the raw curve exhibits several sharp spikes, while the smoothed curve preserves the overall shape and the position for the optimal $\sigma$ fitting.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{figures/smoothing.pdf}
    \caption{Effect of 3-point median smoothing on a raw timing curve.}
    \label{fig:smoothing}
\end{figure}

Each benchmark run recorded three quantities as a function of $\sigma$: the spreading complexity $S(\sigma) = M w(\sigma, \varepsilon)^d$, the FFT complexity $F(\sigma) = \tilde{N}(\sigma) \log \tilde{N}(\sigma)$, and the smoothed measured time $\hat{T}(\sigma)$. All three are overlaid in Figure~\ref{fig:all_results}. The goal of the fitting procedure is to find the value of $\gamma$ for which the model sum $S(\sigma) + \gamma F(\sigma)$ attains its minimum at the same $\sigma$ as the real measurement.

Seven parameter combinations were selected for the calibration benchmarks, covering a representative cross-section of real-world workloads. The combinations were drawn from FINUFFT's GitHub issue tracker and discussion threads, where users report the dimension and floating-point precision that characterise their applications. The chosen combinations are listed in Table~\ref{tab:gamma_configs}.

The loss for a single $(\text{config},\rho)$ pair is defined as follows. Let $\sigma^*_{\text{model}} = \arg\min_\sigma \bigl[S(\sigma) + \gamma F(\sigma)\bigr]$ be the $\sigma$ selected by the model, and let $\sigma^*_{\text{meas}} = \arg\min_\sigma \hat{T}(\sigma)$ be the empirically optimal $\sigma$. The error is the difference in measured time between these two points,
\begin{equation}
    \ell(\gamma) = \hat{T}(\sigma^*_{\text{model}}) - \hat{T}(\sigma^*_{\text{meas}}),
    \label{eq:gamma_loss}
\end{equation}
which is zero when the model chooses the true optimum and positive when it selects a suboptimal $\sigma$. Figure~\ref{fig:gamma_error} illustrates this loss for a single density before any tuning, i.e.\ with $\gamma = 1$, showing the duration between the model-predicted and empirically optimal $\sigma$.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{figures/error.pdf}
    \caption{Error between the real time measurement and the complexity prediction as a function of the upsampling factor $\sigma$.}
    \label{fig:gamma_error}
\end{figure}

To find the optimal $\gamma$, the total loss (summed in squares over all configurations and densities) is minimised by a two-stage sweep: a coarse logarithmic scan over $[10^{-3}, 10^{3}]$ to locate the approximate minimum, followed by a fine linear scan in a neighbourhood of that point.
Figure~\ref{fig:all_results} shows the resulting predicted complexity $S(\sigma) + \gamma F(\sigma)$ overlaid on the timing curves for some configurations and density, confirming that the model minimum is close to the measured minimum across the benchmark set.

\section{Optimal $\sigma$ for specific transform}
\label{sec:sigma_search}

The deployed picker finds $\sigma$ with a ternary search: at each step it evaluates the cost at two interior points $\sigma_1 = \mathrm{lo} + (\mathrm{hi}-\mathrm{lo})/3$ and $\sigma_2 = \mathrm{hi} - (\mathrm{hi}-\mathrm{lo})/3$, keeps the half of the interval containing the smaller value, and repeats until the interval is narrower than a fixed tolerance. This is valid whenever the objective is unimodal on $[1.25, 2.00]$, and Section~\ref{sec:cost_model_intro} suggests it should be: $S$ is non-increasing in $\sigma$ while $F$ is non-decreasing, so their sum has at most one valley.

In practice, though, both $S$ and $F$ are \emph{piecewise-constant} functions of $\sigma$: the kernel width $w$ only takes integer values and only changes at discrete thresholds of $\sigma$, and the fine grid length is rounded up to the next 5-smooth integer, so both terms hold flat plateaus between successive thresholds rather than varying smoothly. %This exposed two correctness gaps in the plain ternary search:

%\begin{itemize}
%    \item \textbf{Tie-breaking on plateaus.} The naive comparison keeps the interval containing $\sigma_1$ only when its cost is \emph{strictly} lower than $\sigma_2$'s. On a flat plateau the two costs are frequently equal, the strict comparison fails, and the search always discards the lower-$\sigma$ half by default -- even when the true minimum lies further left, outside the current window entirely. Loosening the comparison to a non-strict inequality, so that ties are broken toward the lower-$\sigma$ half, corrects the bias but does not fully resolve the issue below.
%    \item \textbf{Boundary blind spot.} The two probe points $\sigma_1, \sigma_2$ are always strictly interior to the current interval, so the search never directly evaluates the interval's own endpoints, $1.25$ and $2.00$. When one of $S$ or $F$ has a value that is uniquely low only exactly at an endpoint -- for instance because the fine-grid rounding jumps to a higher plateau the moment $\sigma$ moves past $1.25$ -- that value is structurally invisible to every interior probe the search performs, and is never recovered even after the tie-breaking fix.
%\end{itemize}
%
%Both defects were confirmed empirically: a case with $\sigma_{\text{low}}$ (Section~\ref{sec:sigma_lower_bound}) trivially satisfied at $\sigma=1.25$, whose cost is unambiguously the lowest of the whole sweep, was nonetheless assigned $\sigma \approx 1.50$ by the unpatched search -- a step-by-step trace of the ternary iterations showed the true minimum being discarded at the very first comparison where the two probes tied on a shared plateau. The fix keeps the loosened tie-break and, after the ternary loop converges, explicitly evaluates $S+\gamma F$ at both endpoints $\{1.25, 2.00\}$ and at the converged interior candidate, returning whichever of the three is actually lowest. Re-running the same case with the corrected search recovers $\sigma = 1.25$, matching the brute-force grid search exactly.

\section{Validation Against Baseline}
\label{sec:validation}

Validation was run against the six representative base configurations listed in Table~\ref{tab:sigma_case_configs} (Appendix~\ref{app:sigma_validation}), each swept over both transform types 1 and 2 and nine densities $\rho \in \{0.125,\ldots,32\}$, for $6 \times 2 \times 9 = 108$ configuration/density/type combinations in total. After excluding the 18 combinations where the baseline $\sigma$ fails the feasibility gate of Section~\ref{sec:sigma_lower_bound} and correcting the search-algorithm defects of Section~\ref{sec:sigma_search}, 31 of the remaining 90 combinations saw the two heuristics select a genuinely different $\sigma$; across those 31, the geometric mean of the speedup is $1.056\times$. The full per-combination results are listed in Table~\ref{tab:sigma_full_results} in Appendix~\ref{app:sigma_validation}.
