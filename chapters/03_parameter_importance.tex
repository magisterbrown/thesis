\chapter{Exploring Parameter Importance}
\label{ch:parameter_importance}

Before attempting to optimize FINUFFT's parameters, it is necessary to understand which parameters actually matter and to what degree. This chapter presents a global sensitivity analysis of FINUFFT's configuration space.  We first identify where transform time is actually spent across the three transform types (Section~\ref{sec:stage_breakdown}), then use a global, Gaussian process based search to rank the tunable parameters by their contribution to performance (Section~\ref{sec:gp_sensitivity}). The resulting ranking motivates the choice of parameters treated in depth in Chapters~\ref{ch:sigma_selection} and~\ref{ch:parameter_tuning}.

\section{FINUFFT's Configuration Parameters}
\label{sec:finufft_parameters}

FINUFFT exposes its tunable behaviour through a single options structure called \texttt{finufft\_opts}. Its fields fall into three groups. Data handling options (\texttt{modeord}, \texttt{spreadinterponly}) change the convention used to order output modes or restrict the call to spreading/interpolation only, and do not by themselves change the amount of work performed. Diagnostic options (\texttt{debug}, \texttt{spread\_debug}, \texttt{showwarn}) control logging and are irrelevant to performance. The remaining, performance relevant options are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{nthreads}: number of threads used for the transform.
    \item \texttt{spread\_sort}: whether nonuniform points are sorted before spreading (\texttt{0} off, \texttt{1} on, \texttt{2} heuristic choice).
    \item \texttt{upsampfac}: the upsampling factor $\sigma$ real number in the range $[1.25;2.00]$, treated in depth in Chapter~\ref{ch:sigma_selection}.
    \item \texttt{spread\_thread} and \texttt{maxbatchsize}: control how work is split across threads when multiple strength vectors share the same nonuniform points (vectorized calls, $n_{\mathrm{tr}} > 1$).
    \item \texttt{spread\_nthr\_atomic}: thread count above which the spreader switches from an OpenMP critical section to atomic updates.
    \item \texttt{spread\_max\_sp\_size} ($S_{\max}$): integer in the range $[1;\frac{M}{nthr}]$, the maximum number of points in a subproblem of the spreader, written $S_{\max}$ in the rest of this thesis, treated in depth in Chapter~\ref{ch:parameter_tuning}.
    \item the sort bin dimensions $b_x$, $b_y$ and $b_z$: integers in the range $[1;N_d]$ the size in fine-grid cells of the boxes the nonuniform points are binned into before spreading. Unlike the other entries in this list they are not fields of \texttt{finufft\_opts} at all but constants in the source, fixed at $16$ along the fast axis and $4$ along the slower ones; they are exposed as options here in order to include them in the search, and are treated in depth in Chapter~\ref{ch:parameter_tuning}.
\end{itemize}
Two further fields, \texttt{spread\_kerevalmeth} and \texttt{spread\_kerpad}, are retained only for binary compatibility and have no effect on current versions of the library. A third, \texttt{spread\_kerformula}, is reserved for internal debugging. All three are therefore excluded from the analysis that follows.

\section{Where Does Time Go? Stage-Level Breakdown}
\label{sec:stage_breakdown}

Every FINUFFT call proceeds through three stages, introduced in Chapter~\ref{ch:background}: \texttt{makeplan}, which precomputes kernel coefficients and creates the FFT plan; \texttt{setpts}, which sorts and preprocesses the nonuniform points; and \texttt{execute}, which performs the actual spreading, FFT, and deconvolution. Before deciding which configuration parameters deserve attention, it is useful to know how much of the total runtime each stage typically accounts for, since a parameter that only affects a negligible stage cannot meaningfully improve overall performance.

Using the benchmark configurations distributed with FINUFFT (covering a range of precisions, tolerances, dimensions, and both extremal values of $\sigma$), each call was timed and the three stage durations were normalised so that they summed to one. Figure~\ref{fig:stage_breakdown} shows the resulting distribution, split by transform type. For type 1 and type 2 transforms, \texttt{execute} dominates, accounting for the large majority of the total time, while \texttt{setpts} contributes a smaller but non-negligible share and \texttt{makeplan} is close to zero in almost every case. Type 3 transforms show a markedly different balance: \texttt{setpts} and \texttt{execute} contribute comparable shares, reflecting the additional preprocessing type 3 transforms require to handle nonuniform points on both sides of the transform.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/parameter_importance/stage_breakdown.pdf}
    \caption{Fraction of total call time spent in each stage (\texttt{makeplan}, \texttt{setpts}, \texttt{execute}), by transform type.}
    \label{fig:stage_breakdown}
\end{figure}

This picture was refined further by instrumenting \texttt{setpts} itself, splitting it into its constituent substeps: precomputing kernel Horner coefficients, evaluating the kernel Fourier series used for deconvolution, and creating the FFT plan. These substeps behave largely as fixed, per-call overheads rather than costs that scale with the number of nonuniform points $M$, which is consistent with \texttt{makeplan} and the fixed portion of \texttt{setpts} contributing a shrinking fraction of total time as $M$ grows.

Together, these observations indicate that parameters affecting the \texttt{execute} stage, and specifically the spreading and FFT stages within it, offer the largest potential impact on performance, with parameters affecting \texttt{setpts} becoming relevant mainly for type 3 transforms or when $M$ is small enough that fixed overheads are not amortised.

\section{Global Sensitivity Analysis via Gaussian-Process Optimization}
\label{sec:gp_sensitivity}

A Gaussian-process-based Bayesian optimizer~\cite{rasmussen2006,snoek2012}, as implemented in Optuna~\cite{optuna2019}, searched the joint parameter space and determined the contribution of each configuration parameter. The search space included \texttt{spread\_max\_sp\_size} ($S_{\max}$), \texttt{spread\_sort}, \texttt{upsampfac}, \texttt{spread\_nthr\_atomic} and the two bin dimensions $b_x$ and $b_y$, sampled from the ranges given in Section~\ref{sec:problem_statement}. For each configuration, the optimizer ran the reference transform of Section~\ref{sec:workload} on all $40$ hardware threads and measured the total time of \texttt{setpts} and \texttt{execute}.

The best configuration found completed the transform in $1.643$\,s, against $2.142$\,s for the library defaults; Table~\ref{tab:gp_best} lists the parameters of both. A single trial can be favoured by noise, so both configurations were re-measured three times each on the same node, which gave the same result. The best configuration agrees with the defaults on $\sigma$ and on sorting, and both use atomic updates at this thread count, so the whole of the margin comes from the remaining two parameters: the subproblem size and the bin dimensions. The subproblem size the search settles on agrees with the sweep of Chapter~\ref{ch:parameter_tuning}, which places the minimum of the spreading stage at $S_{\max} \approx 2000$ for the same transform.

Figure~\ref{fig:gp_histogram} shows the distribution of times across all completed trials on a logarithmic axis, with both configurations of Table~\ref{tab:gp_best} marked. Most trials lie within a factor of a few of the default, and a tail of slow configurations runs past the eight-second cap at which trials were stopped.

The fANOVA percentages are estimates of how sensitive the execution time is to each parameter, not of how much each parameter can improve it. A parameter scores highly when poor choices of it are expensive, since the variance across the search space is dominated by the worst configurations, and the score says nothing about the headroom left above the library default. A low score is therefore not evidence of irrelevance: $S_{\max}$ scores low here, yet Chapter~\ref{ch:parameter_tuning} shows that it has a substantial effect on the spreading stage.

\section{An Alternative Search with OpenTuner}
\label{sec:opentuner}

A Gaussian-process sampler is not the only way to search this space, and it is worth asking how much of the result depends on it. The same six parameters were therefore searched a second time with OpenTuner~\cite{ansel2014opentuner}, a general framework for tuning program parameters by measurement. Where Optuna fits one surrogate model and proposes the point that model considers most promising, OpenTuner runs an ensemble of search techniques at once -- differential evolution, several greedy mutation schemes and a Nelder--Mead simplex~\cite{nelder1965simplex} among them -- and lets a bandit allocate trials to whichever technique is currently producing improvements. It builds no model of the space, so it gives no importance scores, but it is indifferent to the shape of the objective.

The objective and the search space were the same as in Section~\ref{sec:gp_sensitivity}. Each trial ran two repetitions and was abandoned after $20$\,s, which is an order of magnitude above the default configuration's time and so only ever truncates configurations that have already lost. The search was stopped after $147$ trials, of which $44$ were unsorted configurations that hit the cap.

Table~\ref{tab:opentuner_best} gives the configuration it found. Measured three times against the defaults on the same node, it completes the transform in $1.732$\,s against $2.142$\,s. The margin is close to the $23.3\%$ of Section~\ref{sec:gp_sensitivity}, and the two searches agree on where it comes from: sorting on, atomic updates, $\sigma$ at its default, and a subproblem size near $10^3$ rather than the $10^5$ the library selects. They disagree only on the bin dimensions, $30 \times 121$ here against $258 \times 9$ there, which the flat basin of the best twenty trials suggests is the parameter the objective is least sensitive to.

Second search agrees with the Gussian Optimization results. It reaches the same region as the analyses that follow, by a different route and without any model of the space: sorting on, the atomic threshold at its lower value, $\sigma$ within a few percent of its default, a subproblem size two orders of magnitude below the shipped constant(constant value), and bins far taller along the y axis than the defaul $16 \times 4$. The best twenty trials all lie in that region and differ mainly in $b_x$, which varies between $20$ and $41$ with no effect beyond the noise. 


\section{Implications for the Remainder of This Thesis}

The stage breakdown and the global sensitivity analysis together justify the choice of parameters examined in the rest of this thesis. \texttt{upsampfac} is the highest-ranked tunable parameter once the binary sorting choice is fixed. Chapter~\ref{ch:sigma_selection} analyzes its effects on spreading and FFT stages performance. The sort bin dimensions rank next, and Chapter~\ref{ch:parameter_tuning} examines how they change amount of memory traffic druing sorint and spreading stages. \texttt{spread\_max\_sp\_size} ($S_{\max}$) ranks low for this particular workload, but also affects spreading memory traffic and load imbalance that are important at other densities and thread counts, so it too receives a dedicated treatment.
